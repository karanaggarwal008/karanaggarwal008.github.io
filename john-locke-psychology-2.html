Is Mental Illness Over-Diagnosed Now, or Just Better Recognised?

Mental illness is both over-diagnosed and better recognised. Many people label normal stress as depression, while clinicians sometimes miss important factors before diagnosing. These two problems are happening at the same time.

That contradiction is not a mistake. It is the real story.

The rise in mental health diagnoses cannot be explained by only over-diagnosis or only better awareness. Both are true. And that is why the debate keeps going in circles. The case for over-diagnosis There is undeniable evidence that some mental health labels are being used too loosely. People now call everyday stress, sadness, or tiredness “depression.” Social media clips encourage viewers to interpret normal human behaviour—daydreaming, talking too much, occasional forgetfulness—as signs of ADHD. Clinicians report that more patients arrive at appointments with their own self-diagnoses, and some of these are inaccurate. This is real. Researchers call it “concept creep”: terms once reserved for serious disorders are now being applied to mild or fleeting experiences. On platforms like TikTok and Instagram, mental health language is often casual and sometimes incorrect. When that language spreads, people start attributing formal disorders to normal variations in thought and emotion. Overdiagnosis is also happening in clinical settings. A 2024 Lancet editorial noted that psychiatric diagnostic evaluations sometimes fail to separate real disorders from typical variations in human dimensions like thought and emotion. In other words, the line between illness and normal experience is becoming blurry.

This is not speculation. GPs across England have told the BBC that mental health problems are being over-diagnosed, arguing that “life being stressful is not an illness.” Hundreds agree that everyday difficulties are being medicalised.

So over-diagnosis is happening. It is measurable. And it contributes to rising statistics. The case for better recognition But attributing the entire rise to over-diagnosis is a dangerous oversimplification. Some of the increase comes from genuinely improved awareness. Over the past 15 years, public health campaigns have reduced stigma and made people more willing to seek help. When fewer people suffer in silence, more people report symptoms. That creates the appearance of a sharper increase than is actually happening.

A 2026 Guardian article by Oxford psychologist Dr. Suzanne Foul notes that “some of the observed rise may ironically stem from a more accurate and empathetic public awareness of mental health issues.” That is exactly what awareness campaigns intended: to help people acknowledge struggles instead of hiding them.

Better recognition is especially true for groups that were historically underdiagnosed. Women, children, and minority communities were often ignored by mental health systems. Now they are more likely to be assessed and treated. Part of the increase is simply correcting past underdiagnosis.

So better recognition is also happening. It is real. And it also contributes to rising statistics. The overlooked third factor: life is getting harder There is another factor that most people ignore: conditions may genuinely be worsening. Mental health issues can be triggered or aggravated by stress. There is substantial evidence that life has become more challenging over the last 15 years. Financial instability, geopolitical crises, environmental collapse, the ongoing repercussions of the pandemic, drastic cuts to youth community programs, and the prevalence of smartphones and social media have all played a role.

These factors alone cannot fully account for the changes we observe, but they likely contribute significantly. Younger generations report higher rates of anxiety, depression, and burnout. This is not just labelling. It is not just awareness. It is a real increase in distress. So the rise in diagnoses may reflect three things at once: Some over-diagnosis Some better recognition A genuine increase in mental health risk Most debates ignore this third possibility. They treat the issue as binary: either “real” or “fabricated.” But multiple phenomena can occur simultaneously.

My view: it’s both I feel it’s both. Many people just name anything as depression. And very few are actually considering all factors.

That is the real problem.

When people self-diagnose, they often skip the process of ruling out other causes. They do not consider whether symptoms might be temporary, situational, or caused by stress, sleep,

substances, trauma, or medical conditions. They grab the label that feels closest to their experience.

Even when people seek professional help, clinicians sometimes rush to diagnose without thoroughly assessing context. A 2023 study on misdiagnosis and overdiagnosis in criminal justice systems noted that pressure to quickly resolve cases can lead to hasty diagnoses and inaccurate conclusions. That problem exists in regular healthcare too.

So yes, over-diagnosis is happening. Many people are applying serious labels to mild or temporary struggles. But better recognition is also happening. Many people who were previously ignored are now being seen.

The problem is not just over-diagnosis. The problem is shallow diagnosis. Too few people are considering all factors before assigning a label.

The strongest argument against my view A fair counterargument is that focusing on over-diagnosis distracts from the real issue: underdiagnosis in certain populations.

Underdiagnosis is still a major problem where stigma remains high and access to care is limited. Many people—especially in low-income communities, rural areas, or cultures where mental illness is shameful—still suffer without help. In those cases, the rise in diagnoses is not over-diagnosis. It is justice.

This is not a weak objection. It is partly true. Over-diagnosis in some groups does not cancel out underdiagnosis in others. Both can exist simultaneously.

But that does not mean the over-diagnosis problem is irrelevant. It means we must be careful not to dismiss all rising statistics as over-diagnosis. We also must not dismiss all concerns about over-diagnosis as stigma.

Both problems exist. Both must be addressed.

The philosophical problem The deeper issue is that diagnosis is not just a medical act. It is a moral act. When someone is diagnosed with a mental illness, they receive a label that changes how they see themselves, how others see them, and how the system treats them. That label can be helpful. It can provide access to treatment, understanding, and support. But it can also be harmful. It can create dependency, shame, or a fixed identity.

Diagnosis assumes that we can clearly separate illness from normal experience. But that separation is not always clear. Depression can look like sadness. ADHD can look like distractibility. Anxiety can look like worry.

The problem is not that we diagnose too much. The problem is that we diagnose too shallowly.

A diagnosis should not be a quick label. It should be a careful process that considers context, duration, severity, and alternative causes. It should ask: Is this temporary or persistent? Is this situational or general? Is this illness or stress? Is this depression or just grief? Too often, those questions are skipped.

What crisis really means So when I say mental illness is both over-diagnosed and better recognised, I do not mean the statistics are fake. I mean the process behind them is inconsistent. Some people are genuinely receiving diagnoses they need.

Some people are receiving labels they do not need.

Some people who need help are still not receiving it.

That is the real situation.

Mental illness is not simply over-diagnosed. It is not simply better recognised. It is both. And the gap between sincere diagnosis and shallow diagnosis is widening. The danger of simplification The most dangerous thing about this debate is how binary it is framed. People demand a single answer: over-diagnosis OR better recognition. But reality is messier. If we focus only on over-diagnosis, we risk dismissing people in genuine distress. We start calling them “snowflakes” and treating their concerns as fragile exaggerations. That is counterproductive. Being disregarded during a crisis only amplifies distress. If we focus only on better recognition, we risk medicalising normal human experiences. We start treating everyday stress as illness. That weakens the meaning of mental illness and makes it harder for people with severe conditions to get serious attention. Both errors are dangerous. We must avoid both.

The solution The solution is not to stop diagnosing. It is to diagnose more carefully. We need clinicians who assess context thoroughly.

We need schools that teach students to distinguish stress from illness. We need social media that does not encourage self-diagnosis.

We need systems that treat both over-diagnosis and underdiagnosis as real problems.

Original essay · page 5

Most importantly, we need people to understand that mental health labels are not badges. They are tools. And tools must be used correctly.

Conclusion

Mental illness is both over-diagnosed and better recognised.

Many people name anything as depression.

Many people finally receive help they were denied.

Many diagnoses skip important factors.

The rise in diagnoses is not a single problem. It is three problems at once: Some over-diagnosis Some better recognition A genuine increase in distress That is why the debate never ends. Both sides are partly right. Both sides are partly wrong. The real question is not whether mental illness is over-diagnosed or better recognised. The real question is whether we are diagnosing carefully enough.

Because when diagnosis becomes shallow, everyone loses. People with serious conditions lose credibility. People with mild struggles lose clarity. And the system loses trust. Mental illness is both over-diagnosed and better recognised. But the deeper truth is that diagnosis is too often shallow. And that is what needs to change.
