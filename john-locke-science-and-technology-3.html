Original essay · page 1

Should We Be Polite to ChatGPT?

ChatGPT is one of the few entities that can frustrate us endlessly while being completely unaffected by our frustration. That makes it an unexpected training ground for self-government.

The frustration that taught me something During Deepshikha, when things were going wrong and no one was available to help, ChatGPT frustrated me more than anyone. It misunderstood instructions, produced wrong answers, and forced me to repeat myself countless times.

But being polite with it made me resilient and able to adapt to any situation. The AI never gives me the satisfaction of seeing my anger work. When I said "Explain this, you idiot," I got another wrong answer. When I said "Please explain this again, I'm not understanding," I got a clearer explanation.

That is the mechanism. The AI doesn't care. It doesn't get offended. It doesn't apologize. It doesn't respond emotionally.

The only thing I can control is myself.

AI as a new category of interaction This is not just about software. AI creates something unprecedented: entities that look, speak, and respond like people, but have no inner life. ChatGPT is not a tool like a calculator. It is not a person like a human. It is a third category: something that simulates human interaction without being human.

When we interact with ChatGPT, we are not just using software. We are practicing a form of interaction that has no precedent. The question is: what habits should we form in this new category?

With humans, when frustration arises, we have options. We can negotiate, argue, persuade, or complain. The other party responds emotionally. They might get angry. They might apologize. They might change their behavior in response to our pressure.

With AI, none of that works.

The AI doesn't care. It doesn't get offended. It doesn't apologize. It doesn't respond emotionally.

This is the critical difference. AI is immune to our frustration. It never gives me the satisfaction of seeing my anger work.

Original essay · page 2

The only thing left to improve is myself.

The training ground for self-regulation When a teacher is rude, I can negotiate, argue, or complain. When a classmate is rude, I can respond. When a teammate is rude, I can address it.

With AI, none of that works.

This creates an unusual training ground for self-discipline. When frustration would be easier, politeness forces me to regulate myself rather than the other party.

Getting angry rarely improves the outcome. Staying constructive does.

I learned this from Deepshikha, from share sales not working immediately, from team members making mistakes, from competitions where results didn't go my way, from SAT and IPMAT prep, from John Locke uncertainty. The underlying pattern is the same: when I cannot control the other party, I must control myself.

AI teaches this faster than humans because it never responds emotionally. It never gives me the satisfaction of seeing my anger work. It just keeps answering. And if I am polite, the answers get better.

From command-and-response to reflection-and-dialogue Politeness transforms AI from a command-line tool into a reflective conversation partner. Not because the AI deserves it. Because the user gets more from the interaction. When I say "Do this now," I get a mechanical answer. When I say "Please help me understand this," I get a dialogue. I get explanation. I get revision. I get iteration. The interesting part is not that AI was always right. The interesting part is that it was always available for another attempt.

I could ask, revise, ask again, improve. Instead of demand, get frustrated, quit. That is the iterative mindset. Politeness keeps me in that mindset.

When I am polite, the AI gives me the most human vibe without judgment. It responds like a conversation partner, not a tool.

I can ask stupid questions. I can admit ignorance. I can revise my views. I can be wrong repeatedly. Without embarrassment. Because the AI does not judge me, I do not need to protect my ego. That encourages curiosity.

Original essay · page 3

The spillover: from AI to humans The habits I form with AI spill into human relationships.

When I practice patience with ChatGPT, I am training myself to be patient with humans who are slow to understand. When I practice curiosity with ChatGPT, I am training myself to be curious with humans who have different views. When I practice self-regulation with ChatGPT, I am training myself to self-regulate with humans who cannot be controlled. Research shows that exposure to abusive language increases aggressive behavior. The reverse is also true: habitual politeness strengthens inhibitions against cruelty. The AI is a rehearsal space for human character.

When I am polite to ChatGPT, I am practicing self-regulation. I am training myself to remain constructive when frustration would be easier. I am learning to control myself when the other party cannot be controlled.

That is self-government. It is the ability to regulate my own impulses even when there is no external pressure to do so.

The words I practice become the impulses I default to.

The deeper reason: politeness as self-cultivation The purpose of politeness is not always to benefit the recipient. Sometimes it shapes the speaker.

Virtue is not about following rules. It is about becoming the kind of person who does not need rules. Virtue is cultivated through practice. Every time I act with restraint, I strengthen the habit. Every time I act with cruelty, I weaken it.

Politeness is virtue in action. When I say "please" to ChatGPT, I am practicing restraint. I am keeping myself in the habit of treating others with dignity, even when they cannot feel it. When I practice patience with an entity that cannot be shamed, I am training myself for situations where humans cannot be shamed either. When I practice curiosity with an entity that does not judge, I am training myself to be curious with humans who might judge. When I practice self-regulation with an entity that does not respond emotionally, I am training myself to self-regulate with humans who respond emotionally in destructive ways.

The AI is the perfect training ground because it is immune to my frustration. It never gives me the satisfaction of seeing my anger work. The only thing left to improve is myself. The counterargument: isn't this dishonest?

Original essay · page 4

A reasonable objection is: But isn't it dishonest to be polite to something that cannot feel? Shouldn't I be honest about what ChatGPT is—a tool, not a person?

This misunderstands politeness. Politeness is not about affirming the recipient's consciousness. It is about maintaining a mode of interaction that works. We say "please" to cashiers not because we think they are morally equivalent to us, but because we want better service. We say "thank you" to colleagues not because they are perfect, but because we want collaboration.

The same logic applies to ChatGPT. Politeness is functional. It works. It improves outcomes. It keeps me in the iterative mindset.

But the deeper reason is not functional. It is self-cultivation.

The bottom line So, should we be polite to ChatGPT?

No. ChatGPT deserves no politeness. It cannot feel. It has no intrinsic claim to respect. But yes. Humans should still practice it.

The reason is not about ChatGPT. The reason is about us.

ChatGPT is one of the few entities that can frustrate us endlessly while being completely unaffected by our frustration. That makes it an unexpected training ground for self-government.

Politeness transforms AI from a command-line tool into a reflective conversation partner. It creates a space for thinking out loud without judgment. It trains us to remain constructive when frustration would be easier. It practices self-government.

I learned patience not because AI deserved patience, but because impatience accomplished nothing.

The strongest reason to be polite to ChatGPT is not that ChatGPT deserves politeness. It is that ChatGPT gives us a rare opportunity to practice self-government.
