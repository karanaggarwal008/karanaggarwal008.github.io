When Measures Become Targets: The Unintended Transformation of Education

I argued that when credentials, grades and prestige become the targets of education, students and institutions can end up optimising the signals rather than the underlying learning. Measuring success can change the very behaviour being measured.

My competition essay develops this argument in full, including its qualifications and references.

When Measures Become Targets: The Unintended Transformation of Education Meritocratic educational policy failed not because it measured the wrong things, but because any measure tied to opportunity eventually becomes a target.

Policymakers assumed educational credentials would measure human development and allocate opportunity fairly.

Instead, credentials changed how people pursued development itself.[ web.stanford ]

The most devastating unintended consequence of modern meritocratic educational policy was the transformation of education from a process of developing human capital into a system for signaling human capital.

This consequence is especially grave because it does not merely produce a bad outcome at one point in time.

It reorganizes how millions of young people spend their most formative years, distorts how societies allocate talent, and reproduces itself across generations through institutions that still appear fair on the surface.[ nber ]

Human capital The original policy logic was not foolish.

Human capital theory treated education as an investment that raises productivity, earnings, and long-run economic growth, so using educational achievement as a gateway to opportunity appeared both efficient and just.

If schools and universities helped individuals become more capable, then rewarding educational achievement seemed a reasonable way to replace inherited privilege with merit.[ journals.sagepub ]

This expectation also fits a broader moral vision.

Meritocracy promised that advancement would depend less on family background and more on effort and demonstrated ability.

In that framework, credentials looked like neutral evidence of development rather than instruments that could reshape motivation itself.[ semanticscholar ]

Signaling logic That assumption broke down once credentials became the dominant route to jobs, income, and status.

Michael Spence's signaling theory begins from a simple fact: employers cannot directly observe productive capability at the moment of hiring, so they rely on signals that are easier to observe, including education.

Once education functions as a signal under conditions of uncertainty, individuals have a strong incentive to acquire the signal even when the signal is only imperfectly connected to genuine learning.[ sfu ]

This is the turning point of the essay.

The policy was meant to reward development.

But as soon as credentials became gateways, they also became targets.

Students then had rational reasons to optimize grades, test scores, institution prestige, and approved extracurricular profiles rather than intellectual growth itself.

The deeper failure was not simply that policymakers used imperfect measures.

It was that they underestimated how measures change behaviour when access to opportunity depends on them.[ pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih ]

Bryan Caplan pushes this tension further by arguing that much of education functions less as skill formation than as certification of intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity, which employers value regardless of whether schooling substantially raises productivity.

Even if one rejects Caplan's stronger conclusions, his argument clarifies the policy danger: a system justified in human capital terms can increasingly operate in signaling terms without openly admitting the shift.[ en.wikipedia ]

Inflation and competition Once credentials became valuable signals, inflation followed.

Randall Collins argued that educational expansion did not simply broaden opportunity.

It also created a cycle in which more schooling was required to secure positions that previously demanded less, turning credentials into a central mechanism of social stratification.

As degrees proliferate, they lose distinctiveness, so individuals chase still higher or more prestigious credentials to preserve relative advantage.[ archive ]

That dynamic is not merely economic.

It is positional.

Fred Hirsch's framework of positional goods explains why competition intensifies even when average educational attainment rises: the value of certain goods depends on relative standing, not absolute abundance.

Education increasingly behaves this way when what matters is not simply whether one is educated, but where one ranks, which institution one enters, and how one compares with peers in a crowded queue for scarce status positions.[ sciencedirect ]

This is why the system does not stabilize.

It escalates.

Bills describes schooling as a world of congested credentials, where value is increasingly positional rather than developmental.

Tomlinson finds that students themselves perceive a congested graduate labor market in which the degree alone is no longer enough, forcing them into escalating efforts to distinguish themselves.

What began as a policy for fairer allocation gradually becomes a competition in which everyone must run faster merely to stand still.[ ideas.repec ]

Why prediction failed The hardest part of the question is not identifying the unintended consequence.

It is explaining why intelligent policymakers failed to foresee it.

The answer lies in a series of conceptual blind spots.

First, policymakers confused measurement with motivation.

Human capital theory encouraged them to see education as investment in skill, so they assumed that rewarding educational success would encourage more development.

But once educational success became a high-stakes gateway, individuals quite predictably optimized for whatever the system visibly rewarded.[ nber ]

Second, policymakers underestimated positional competition.

Hirsch's insight matters here because positional races do not end when supply expands.

They simply move upward.

When more people obtain degrees, competition shifts toward more selective institutions, postgraduate credentials, branded extracurriculars, and finer distinctions in prestige.

In practice, however, the competitive pressure is merely intensifying and relocating.[ taylorfrancis ]

Third, policymakers misunderstood educational goals.

David Labaree's work is indispensable because he shows that education is pulled between public purposes and private advancement.

In his account, democratic equality and social efficiency treat education as a public good, while social mobility treats it as a private good for getting ahead.

Once social mobility eclipses the other goals, students naturally focus less on becoming educated and more on converting schooling into advantage.

His sharper formulation is devastating: the process of getting ahead often interferes with getting an education.[ eric.ed ]

That line captures the core of the prediction failure.

Policymakers thought credentials would record development after it occurred.

They did not fully grasp that credentials would redirect development before it occurred.

The system did not merely sort talent.

It changed the form that ambition itself took.[ pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih ]

Why most devastating This claim has to be earned.

Many unintended consequences in public policy are severe, but most are limited by sector, geography, or time horizon.

This one is unusually devastating because it penetrates the formation of persons, the allocation of talent, and the reproduction of status all at once.[ hbs ]

It shapes persons because it enters life early and colonizes formative years.

Under a credential regime, students increasingly learn to ask not what is worth understanding, but what will count.

Curiosity becomes subordinate to optimization.

Identity forms around admissibility, rankings, and externally legible achievement.

That is not just stress.

It is a distortion of the purposes around which young people organize effort and selfhood.[ web.stanford ]

It misallocates talent because employers often use credentials as proxies when they cannot directly observe capability.

Fuller and Raman show that degree inflation has spread to jobs whose current incumbents often do not hold the newly required degrees, demonstrating that employers increasingly default to credentials as screening devices rather than direct measures of skill.

When this happens at scale, societies steer effort toward signal production rather than capability production.[ sfu ]

It reproduces inequality because elite hiring often rewards prestige signals that are themselves socially uneven.

Rivera shows that elite employers privilege super-elite institutions and high-status extracurriculars, reinforcing closure at the top of the labor market.

A system introduced to weaken inherited advantage can therefore rebuild hierarchy in meritocratic language, making the resulting inequality more difficult to challenge because it appears earned.[ asanet ]

It sustains itself institutionally because each participant has rational reasons to continue.

Students chase credentials because the stakes are real.

Parents support the race because opting out is risky.

Employers keep using signals because direct assessment is costly.

Schools intensify measurement because measurable performance is administratively legible.

The system persists not because everyone misunderstands it, but because each actor adapts rationally within it.[ hbs ]

Evidence of distortion The secondary consequences matter because they reveal the scale of the primary one.

The growth of shadow education and admissions consulting shows how much effort is now devoted to signal optimization around gateways that are supposed to reflect merit.

The market exists because the signal matters enough to justify strategic investment in mastering it.[ growthmarketreports ]

Student mental health evidence is also relevant, though it should remain secondary.

Large-scale surveys show high prevalence of mental health problems among college students, which is consistent with a system that places sustained pressure on performance under competitive evaluation.

This does not prove the whole thesis on its own, but it strengthens the broader claim that a regime of signaling competition reshapes student life far beyond the classroom.[ pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih ]

Counterargument A serious essay must concede that credential-based allocation remains fairer than nepotism or inherited privilege.

Meritocracy did produce moral gains.

It offered a more defensible basis for selection than birth alone.

But a policy can be superior to its alternatives and still produce a devastating unintended consequence.

The question is not whether meritocratic allocation is better than hereditary privilege.

The question is whether the chosen mechanism transformed education in ways policymakers failed to foresee.

It did.[ web.stanford ]

Conclusion

The deepest failure of meritocratic educational policy was not that it rewarded achievement.

It was that it tied opportunity to measures in ways that guaranteed those measures would become targets.

Once that happened, education was gradually transformed from a process of developing talent into a competition to signal talent.[ archive ]

That is why this consequence is so devastating.

It reaches into the motives of students, the selection practices of employers, and the status structure of society at the same time.

The policy was meant to measure development.

Instead, it changed how development was pursued.

That is the prediction failure at the heart of the essay, and it is why this unintended consequence deserves to be taken seriously as one of the most consequential of the modern meritocratic state.[ ideas.repec ]
