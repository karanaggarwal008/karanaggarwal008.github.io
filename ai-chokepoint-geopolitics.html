AI Chokepoint Geopolitics

My argument about the infrastructure, supply chains and political power behind frontier AI.

When Washington tightened restrictions on advanced AI chip exports, it was not simply slowing China’s access to the newest hardware. It was revealing the real geography of AI power: a system in which strategic advantage flows less from algorithms than from the ability to command the infrastructure that makes those algorithms trainable at scale.

“AI chokepoint geopolitics” is the sharpest name for that system. “Compute colonialism” gestures in the right direction, but it invites a historical analogy that is easier to dispute than the underlying structure: frontier intelligence now depends on a small number of firms, fabs, cloud platforms, and states that can grant or deny access to the means of computation.

The concentration starts with chips and expands into the rest of the stack. OECD work on AI infrastructure shows bottlenecks at nearly every layer: advanced semiconductors, cloud capacity, networking, power, and cooling. Nvidia’s position in AI accelerators remains central to that stack, but the more revealing fact is that its chips sit inside a supply chain still heavily dependent on Taiwan’s fabrication ecosystem, above all TSMC. The World Bank has estimated that Nvidia controls 70 to 95 percent of the market for AI chips, a range broad enough to allow competition at the margins and narrow enough to reveal the center of gravity. TSMC itself has said the global chip market could reach $1.5 trillion by 2030 as AI drives growth, a reminder that the market is expanding even as control over its core nodes remains highly concentrated.

That concentration matters because it turns industrial capacity into strategic leverage. When Reuters reported in 2026 that Washington was considering new AI chip export rules tied to foreign investments in American data centers, the policy logic was unmistakable: access to advanced compute can be conditioned, negotiated, and used as leverage. The United States is treating chips less like ordinary goods than like a controllable strategic resource. OECD analysis of AI supply chains reaches a similar conclusion from another angle, arguing that the physical infrastructure underpinning AI is unusually susceptible to market power and lock-in because so much of it is difficult to replicate quickly at scale.

China has responded accordingly. Its push to reduce dependence on Nvidia and accelerate domestic chip capacity is not simply a response to commercial rivalry; it is an attempt to escape infrastructure vulnerability. The AI contest is often described as a race to build better models, but the more consequential struggle may be over who can set the terms under which those models are possible in the first place. In that sense, compute is not just an input. It is a chokepoint that turns industrial policy into geopolitics.

Taiwan sits at the center of that vulnerability. TSMC’s fabrication capacity remains indispensable to the global AI economy, and Reuters has repeatedly noted how robust AI demand has propelled the company’s growth and driven continued investment in Taiwan, Arizona, and Japan. The dispersion of some capacity does not remove the strategic fact that the most advanced nodes still depend on a tightly clustered production base. The world may buy chips globally; it still fabricates many of the most important ones through a narrow geographic corridor. For now, that is how frontier compute is made.

This is why compute concentration is more than a market story. It reshapes bargaining power. A state that cannot secure frontier-grade chips, cloud access, or advanced packaging does not merely face higher costs. It enters AI from a weaker position. OECD work on competition in AI infrastructure describes a market where firms can lock in users through integrated services and high switching costs, making dependence sticky once it forms. The logic travels easily across borders. Countries and firms on the outside can adopt AI quickly, but they do so on terms defined elsewhere.

The Global South should not be reduced to a simple story of exclusion. The deeper issue is capability stratification. India has software depth and a large digital economy, yet frontier compute remains a hard constraint on moving from AI consumption to frontier training. Brazil, Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Indonesia face a similar asymmetry: they may build applications, regulate deployments, and adapt imported systems, but the architecture of frontier development is still being set by actors outside their borders. That creates sovereignty constraints subtler than outright denial. It means national AI strategies can be ambitious on paper while remaining operationally dependent in practice.

This is also why “sovereignty” in AI often sounds stronger than it is. A country may announce a compute strategy, but if it lacks enough domestic training capacity, it remains exposed to foreign pricing decisions, cloud terms, and export rules. Sovereignty without compute is incomplete sovereignty. The phrase captures a political ambition that cannot become fully real unless it is backed by infrastructure.

The standard counterarguments deserve real weight. Cloud access has democratized experimentation, and it would be wrong to deny the practical gains that come from renting rather than owning infrastructure. Open-source models have also widened participation, while edge computing can move some inference closer to users and reduce dependence on centralized datacenters for specific tasks. These are genuine decentralizing forces. They matter especially for startups, universities, and smaller economies that could not otherwise enter the field at all.

Yet democratization at the user level is not the same as power at the frontier. Open models still require chips, electricity, cooling, and capital. Edge systems still depend on the same semiconductor ecosystem for training and improvement. Nature reported in 2025 that data centers accounted for about 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption in 2024 and that this could more than double by 2030, with the United States, Europe, and China already responsible for roughly 85 percent of current data-center energy use. Stanford HAI’s 2025 AI Index adds a second anchor: global private AI investment reached record levels, but remained heavily concentrated in a small number of economies and firms. A technology that increasingly depends on energy, land, capital, and permitting is not dematerializing; it is becoming more industrial.

That is the point the current moment keeps clarifying. Export controls are no longer an afterthought to AI competition; they are one of its main instruments. Taiwan is not simply a manufacturing location; it is a geopolitical hinge. Nvidia is not just a winning company; it is part of an upstream architecture that shapes who can participate in the next generation of intelligence systems. For countries outside the core semiconductor-cloud complex, the issue is not whether AI arrives. It already has. The issue is whether it arrives as a capability they can shape or as an infrastructure they must rent.

The deeper lesson is simple, and it is not flattering to the idea that digital technologies naturally flatten power. AI is often sold as software, but its geopolitics are increasingly industrial. The states and firms that control compute are not merely selling access to machines. They are quietly defining the terms of intelligence itself. In the AI era, power belongs less to those who write the most elegant model than to those who can command the infrastructure that lets a model exist at all.
