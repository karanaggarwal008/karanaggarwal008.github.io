Is trial by jury obsolete?

Trial by jury is not completely obsolete.

In ordinary cases, it can still serve an important purpose.

But in a world shaped by smartphones, social media, and nonstop publicity, it is becoming increasingly obsolete in the cases where fairness matters most.

The problem is not that ordinary citizens are incapable of judgment.

The problem is that jury trials depend on conditions that modern life no longer reliably provides: insulation, patience, and the ability to decide a case only on what is said in court.

The classic argument for trial by jury is powerful.

A jury prevents justice from becoming the property of the state alone.

It allows ordinary citizens to take part in the administration of law.

It acts as a democratic check on professional judges and prosecutors.

It also carries an important symbolic value: a defendant is not judged only by legal authority, but by fellow members of the community.

That ideal still has force.

A jury can bring varied perspectives into the courtroom.

It can reduce the risk that a single judge's assumptions decide the case.

It can make the criminal process feel less remote and more publicly legitimate.

This is why the question is not simple.

Jury trial is not an outdated superstition.

It is rooted in real political and legal values.

Yet the strength of the ideal depends on a deeper assumption: that jurors can be meaningfully separated from outside influence.

That assumption was always imperfect, but it is far weaker now.

The jury system was built for a world in which outside opinion could be limited, delayed, and geographically contained.

Today, public narrative is immediate, portable, and constant.

A juror does not need to seek outside information; outside information now seeks the juror.

This matters because a fair jury is not simply a random group of people.

It is a group of people able to decide only on admissible evidence.

That distinction is crucial.

Random selection may make a jury socially broad, but it does not make it immune to bias.

In fact, because jurors are ordinary citizens, they are especially exposed to the same forces that shape everyone else: headlines, online outrage, political framing, emotional video clips, and algorithmically amplified narratives.

The very feature that once gave juries democratic legitimacy—their ordinariness—now makes them more vulnerable to the informational chaos of the modern public sphere.

High-profile cases reveal the problem most clearly.

In such trials, the legal case no longer unfolds only inside the courtroom.

It unfolds on television, in comment sections, in viral posts, and across thousands of small judgments made by the public before the trial even begins.

By the time a juror is selected, the accused may already exist in public consciousness as a villain, victim, symbol, or cause.

Even if jurors sincerely promise impartiality, that promise becomes harder to believe when the case has already been socially narrated for weeks or months.

Psychological research strengthens this concern.

Pretrial publicity has been found to exert small but significant effects on legal judgments, pushing jurors toward the side favored by the publicity.

That effect does not need to be dramatic to be dangerous.

A criminal conviction does not become acceptable because the unfair influence was subtle.

If publicity shifts a jury even slightly in a close case, the fairness of the verdict is already compromised.

The digital age deepens the problem in two ways.

First, publicity is now much more pervasive.

Jurors are not influenced only by major newspapers or television reports.

They are influenced by fragments: memes, short videos, commentary accounts, reposted claims, amateur "evidence breakdowns," and emotional reactions from strangers.

Second, exposure is no longer confined to the period before trial.

Even during trial, jurors return home carrying a device that connects them instantly to ongoing public discussion.

Courts may instruct jurors not to research the case or discuss it online, but instructions cannot erase curiosity, accidental exposure, or subconscious influence.

This is why the strongest modern objection to jury trial is not that jurors are foolish.

It is that the institution asks for a kind of informational isolation that contemporary life is bad at supplying.

A jury system that depends on jurors being untouched by the world is badly matched to a world that never stops talking.

In earlier periods, the courtroom could plausibly dominate the juror's mental environment during the trial.

Today, the courtroom must compete with the internet.

Defenders of jury trial can respond in several ways.

They can argue that judges are also biased, and that replacing juries with judges does not solve the problem.

They can also argue that juries still provide democratic legitimacy in a way professional fact-finders cannot.

Both points are fair.

A judge is not a neutral machine, and public participation in justice remains valuable.

But these arguments do not fully answer the modern problem.

The question is not whether judges are perfect.

The question is whether juries still possess the special legitimacy they are supposed to offer when the process that produces their verdict is increasingly contaminated by outside influence.

Another defense is that courts have adapted.

Federal courts, for example, have strengthened model jury instructions warning jurors not to use social media or conduct online research.

This shows that the legal system recognizes the danger.

But it also shows how serious the danger has become.

An institution is in trouble when it must repeatedly warn its participants not to use the most ordinary tools of daily life in order to preserve its fairness.

The very existence of these warnings is evidence that the old model of juror isolation no longer fits normal human behavior.

There is also a more philosophical problem.

Trial by jury assumes that fact-finding can be separated from public narrative.

But in the digital age, narrative now precedes fact.

People often encounter interpretation before they encounter evidence.

In high-profile cases, the accused is framed morally before being assessed legally.

That makes the jury less a finder of

fact than a final stage in a larger public drama.

Once that happens, the ideal of calm, evidence-based deliberation is weakened from the start.

For that reason, I would not argue that jury trial is obsolete in all cases.

That claim is too broad and too easy to defeat.

In low-profile cases, where publicity is limited and jurors are less likely to carry strong prior narratives into court, jury trial may still function reasonably well.

It can still check state power and embody public judgment.

But in high-profile cases, the institution becomes far harder to defend.

Those are precisely the cases in which publicity is most intense, emotions are strongest, and public narrative is least avoidable.

This leads to a more precise conclusion: trial by jury is becoming conditionally obsolete.

It is not obsolete because democratic participation in justice has lost value.

It is obsolete because the practical conditions required for fair jury decision-making are disappearing in exactly the cases where the stakes are highest.

The institution's principle remains attractive; its environment has changed.

If that is right, the solution is not necessarily to abolish juries everywhere.

It is to stop assuming that one model fits every case.

High-profile cases may require greater use of judge-alone trials, stricter sequestration, more aggressive venue changes, or more skeptical standards about whether an impartial jury can truly be found.

The point is not to reject public participation as such.

It is to preserve fairness when publicity makes that participation unreliable.

So, is trial by jury obsolete?

Not completely.

But it is increasingly obsolete in the very cases where the justice system most wants to display fairness and public legitimacy.

The jury was designed for a world in which jurors could be separated from noise.

Our world is built on noise.

That does not make citizens incapable of judgment.

It makes impartial judgment much harder to preserve.

And when an institution depends on conditions that no longer exist, obsolescence is no longer a rhetorical insult.

It becomes a real possibility.
