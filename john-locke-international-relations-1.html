The Best Foreign Aid Is Aid That Makes Itself Unnecessary

I explored aid through a long-run institutional question: does assistance create the conditions for independence, or does it make continued assistance necessary? I was interested in when foreign aid genuinely strengthens local capacity.

My competition essay develops this argument in full, including its qualifications and references.

The Best Foreign Aid Is Aid That Makes Itself Unnecessary Foreign aid neither inherently helps nor hurts poor people. Its impact depends on whether it builds local capacity or substitutes for it. The best foreign aid is aid that makes itself unnecessary — aid that enables people and institutions to solve problems themselves, rather than aid that creates dependency, weakens accountability, or replaces local initiative. econpapers.repec The debate over foreign aid persists because both sides are measuring different things. Supporters measure lives saved. Critics measure institutional development. Both are right because they are measuring different dimensions of the same problem. This is the paradox that explains why aid has both undeniable successes and catastrophic failures. jp-dube The two sides of the paradox The supporters' evidence is undeniable.

PEPFAR, the US HIV/AIDS program, has saved 26 million lives and supported antiretroviral treatment for 20.6 million people worldwide as of December 2024. mcgill GAVI, the Vaccine Alliance, has helped immunize over 1 billion children since 2000, preventing more than 18.8 million future deaths. faculty.chicagobooth Global immunization efforts have saved an estimated 154 million lives over the past 50 years, with 101 million of those lives being infants. chicagobooth Smallpox was eradicated in 1977 through vaccination. Since that date, there has not been a single case of smallpox. chicagobooth These are not abstract claims. They are measurable outcomes. A vaccination delivered from abroad still protects a child. Antiretroviral treatment funded externally still prolongs life. In these cases, the case for aid does not rest on romantic hopes about transformation. It rests on direct intervention against identifiable suffering. faculty.chicagobooth The critics' evidence is equally undeniable.

Long-term aid can create a vicious cycle of dependency, corruption, market distortion, and further poverty. Rather than enabling self-sustaining development, aid can entrench the very weaknesses it was supposed to cure. Governments that rely on donors for resources may become more accountable to external agencies than to their own citizens. par.nsf When donors implement projects that local governments would have undertaken anyway, aid can prevent local bureaucracies from building durable in-house capacity. Aid can fail not only by doing nothing, but by doing too much of the wrong kind. jp-dube

Both sides are right. The question is not whether aid works in the abstract. The question is what the aid is trying to build. jp-dube The tension: short-term success vs long-term failure Foreign aid is most successful when judged by short-term humanitarian outcomes and least successful when judged by long-term political development.

This is the tension that explains why aid has both undeniable successes and catastrophic failures. PEPFAR and GAVI succeed because they measure lives saved. They are not trying to build institutions. They are trying to prevent death. And they succeed brilliantly. mcgill But when aid tries to build institutions, it often fails. When aid substitutes for taxation, it weakens accountability. When aid substitutes for local initiative, it weakens capacity. When aid substitutes for political accountability, it creates dependency. par.nsf The supporters are measuring short-term humanitarian outcomes. Aid saves lives. It prevents death. It treats disease. It builds infrastructure. By these measures, aid works brilliantly. econpapers.repec The critics are measuring long-term political development. Aid creates dependency. It weakens institutions. It corrupts accountability. It replaces local initiative. By these measures, aid often fails. jp-dube Both are right. The question is what the aid is trying to build. jp-dube The framework: humanitarian aid versus development aid Humanitarian aid and development aid should be judged by different metrics. This is the resolution to the paradox. When the two conflict, we must ask: what type of aid is this? And what metric should we use?

Emergency medical aid should be judged by lives saved. If an aid program saves 5 million lives but weakens institutional development, we should still support it. Because in this case, the metric is human survival, not institutional capacity. The value of preventing death is immediate and absolute. The cost of weakened institutions is diffuse and long-term. Development aid should be judged by institutional capacity created. If an aid program builds institutions but does not save lives in the short term, we should support it only if it strengthens local capacity. Because in this case, the metric is sustainability, not humanitarian relief. The value of building institutions is long-term and structural. The cost of delayed relief is immediate but not absolute.

The best foreign aid is aid that makes itself unnecessary. Humanitarian aid makes itself unnecessary by ending the emergency. Vaccination programs eradicate disease. HIV treatment prevents death. Once the emergency ends, the aid stops. Development aid makes

itself unnecessary by building capacity. Education programs raise literacy. Health infrastructure strengthens clinics. Governance programs build accountability. Once capacity is built, the aid stops. econpapers.repec When humanitarian aid and development aid are confused, the paradox becomes worse. When humanitarian aid is judged by institutional metrics, it looks like failure. When development aid is judged by humanitarian metrics, it looks like failure. The solution is not to reject one metric. The solution is to apply the right metric to the right type of aid. jp-dube The original promise: poverty traps Foreign aid was created with a moral vision. After World War II, wealthy nations believed that transferring resources to poorer countries would accelerate development. The logic was straightforward: poverty stems from lack of capital. If rich countries provided money, poor countries could build infrastructure, educate citizens, and grow economies. econpapers.repec Jeffrey Sachs expresses this position through the idea of the poverty trap. The poorest countries often lack even the minimum capital necessary to gain a foothold on the ladder of development. Targeted outside assistance can provide that first push, after which self-sustaining growth may begin. This is not romantic hope. It is structural realism. Some countries are so poor that they cannot invest in health, education, or infrastructure without external help. econpapers.repec This is why Sachs remains important even for critics of aid optimism. His strongest arguments are not the broadest ones. They are the ones tied to concrete poverty traps in health, disease, and basic infrastructure. If a community cannot escape disease burdens or severe malnutrition because it lacks the minimum capital required to do so, then external assistance may be a necessary first step toward later self-reliance. econpapers.repec The critics: when aid substitutes William Easterly argues that large-scale aid often fails because outside planners lack local knowledge and reliable feedback. When projects are designed from distant capitals, those making decisions are frequently too far from the people they claim to help. Without accountability to local realities, aid becomes technocratic rather than responsive. econpapers.repec Easterly's criticism is not simply that aid is inefficient. It is that aid often assumes that development can be engineered from above. True victories against poverty are more often achieved through indigenous, ground-level problem solving than through sweeping external plans. econpapers.repec Dambisa Moyo pushes the criticism further. Her point is not merely that some aid fails. It is that long-run dependence on external finance can corrode the very incentives required for domestic development. Once aid becomes a substitute for political accountability, productive taxation, or local initiative, it stops being a ladder and becomes a crutch. par.nsf

The World Bank literature on aid dependence supports part of this concern. When donors implement projects that local governments would have undertaken anyway, aid can prevent local bureaucracies from building durable in-house capacity. Aid can fail not only by doing nothing, but by doing too much of the wrong kind. jp-dube The real distinction: capacity-building versus substitution The crucial question is not whether aid helps or hurts. The question is whether aid builds local capacity or substitutes for it.

Capacity-building aid helps local people and institutions solve problems themselves. It leaves behind skills, systems, infrastructure, habits of administration, or improved human capital that endure after the donor leaves. Education assistance can raise literacy, teacher quality, and administrative competence. Public health aid can strengthen clinics, supply chains, and trained personnel. Well-designed aid does not merely deliver a service. It increases the recipient society's ability to provide that service in the future. econpapers.repec Substitutive aid does the opposite. It solves an immediate problem while weakening the incentives or structures needed for long-term self-reliance. If foreign contractors design, fund, administer, and monitor everything, local bureaucracies may never learn to do these things well. If governments depend on aid rather than taxation, citizens lose leverage and rulers lose accountability. If donor projects exist outside domestic systems, they may deliver short-term outputs while fragmenting the institutions that should eventually carry the burden. par.nsf This distinction explains the paradox. Aid that builds capacity often succeeds. Aid that substitutes for capacity often fails. jp-dube What successful aid looks like Examples from local-first development literature illustrate the capacity-building approach. Peace Direct argues that effective assistance often comes from "unlocking the power to get things done" through local actors rather than imposing external blueprints. Their case studies emphasize locally led programs in which communities identify needs, adapt resources, and sustain outcomes after donors leave. econpapers.repec A similar logic appears in community health partnerships. In Haiti, local actors confronted the flood of post-earthquake aid by asking how outside support could reinforce local problem-solving rather than overwhelm it. The answer was not rejecting aid, but redesigning it around partnership and capacity. This is not an anti-aid argument. It is an anti-substitution argument. ideas.repec Even official aid agencies increasingly use the language of self-reliance. Recent policy discussions on foreign assistance emphasize promoting self-reliance rather than perpetual dependence, arguing that assistance should strengthen domestic systems so that countries can meet their own needs over time. The fact that aid institutions themselves now frame success in these terms suggests that the central distinction is not ideological. It is practical. jp-dube

The best answer So does foreign aid help or hurt poor people? The best answer is neither, taken by itself. Foreign aid is not a single mechanism with a single effect. It can save lives, expand human capability, and support recovery from severe deprivation. It can also entrench dependency, weaken institutions, and relieve governments of responsibility toward their own citizens. jp-dube The decisive question is what the aid is trying to build. If it builds local capacity, strengthens institutions, and leaves societies more able to solve their own problems, then aid can be not only helpful but transformative. If it substitutes for local initiative, weakens accountability, or turns development into a donor-managed project, then aid may hurt even while appearing to help. econpapers.repec The best foreign aid is aid that makes itself unnecessary. Foreign aid neither inherently helps nor hurts poor people. Dependency does. jp-dube References
