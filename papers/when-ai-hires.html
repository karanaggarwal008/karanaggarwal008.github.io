When AI Hires

A comparative analysis of AI governance using résumé-audit evidence.

Publication stage & contribution

Working research

First and corresponding author with Ridhika Singh

A technology with institutional consequences

Ridhika Singh and I developed this project through the Incognito Blueprints Research Bootcamp. We asked how AI use in hiring could produce biased outcomes and how governance differs across India, the European Union and the United States.

The question kept expanding into the relationship between a technical decision and the institution responsible for it. Some of our discussions went beyond four hours because we kept finding another issue to understand.

Evidence and methodological direction

The manuscript uses comparative policy analysis and reanalysis of existing résumé-audit evidence. Our discussions also considered the literature-review framing and possible future survey work. Those later ideas are distinct from claiming a new survey was already completed.

The important boundary is between interpreting existing evidence and collecting new participant data. The dossier does not present a newly conducted hiring experiment.

Feedback and credit

Ridhika’s contribution shaped the methodological discussion. Karan Dhingra and Saloni Verma provided feedback, and Shrestha Hardia supported the project as commitment mentor.

We were recognised as Young Student Innovators and selected for publication support. Publication support is a development opportunity; I keep the research labelled as working research rather than a published article.

Two kinds of audit evidence

The working manuscript distinguishes traditional correspondence experiments from audits of AI résumé-screening systems. In a correspondence experiment, otherwise matched applications vary a demographic signal and the researchers observe employer responses. A controlled AI audit instead observes how a model treats résumés in a defined screening task. Both can reveal unequal treatment, but they do not observe the same process.

Our methodological discussion centres on keeping those outcomes identifiable. A callback gap concerns an early stage of recruitment; a model preference in a simulated screen is not an observed employment decision. Treating them as one interchangeable measure would hide the distinction that motivates the governance question.

From an observed disparity to responsibility

Commercial systems can make it difficult for an external researcher to observe training data, scoring rules or model logic. The project therefore examines what observable outcomes can tell us and which questions remain about the people and institutions responsible for the decision.

The comparative policy component considers India, the European Union and the United States. The purpose is to connect the evidence with questions of coverage, accountability and enforcement. This portfolio describes the research question and approach; the manuscript remains under development, including the treatment of existing audit evidence and the framing of the review.
