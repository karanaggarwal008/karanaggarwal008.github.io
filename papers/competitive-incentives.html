When competition destroys value

Competitive Incentives and the Persistence of Short-Term Value Destruction in Firm Strategy.

Publication stage & contribution

SSRN preprint

Solo author

An outcome that can look irrational

I developed this case analysis from earlier business-related writing and LinkedIn posts. The central concern is the persistence of short-term value destruction when firms respond to competitive incentives.

Rather than assuming that a poor collective outcome means every decision-maker failed to think, the paper asks what the competitive setting encourages individual firms to do.

Form and scope

The work is a case analysis circulated as an SSRN preprint, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6034116. It is distinct from an empirical study that estimates an effect across a newly collected sample.

The contribution is an argument about incentives and strategy. I connect it with my case-competition and branding work, where a recommendation has to account for how other actors may respond.

Reading the original

The public preprint preserves the complete case reasoning. It remains labelled as a preprint in this portfolio.

That publication stage allows the idea to be read and discussed without implying that it has received final journal acceptance.

The case: Swiggy and Zomato

The preprint uses Indian food delivery to examine discount competition. In the case, low switching costs and price sensitivity make customer acquisition and order volume salient competitive signals. Scale can also affect bargaining power with restaurants and delivery partners. A firm considering reducing discounts therefore has to consider both its margin and its relative position.

The argument is that promotional spending can persist even when its costs are recognised. If a firm expects unilateral restraint to lose customers or weaken its position, continuing to discount can be a defensible response to the competitive environment. The paper examines that mechanism through a case analysis, rather than estimating the causal contribution of discounts to company profitability.

Immediate penalties, delayed rewards

I distinguish the immediacy of a visible short-term penalty from the uncertainty of a long-term reward. Order volume, growth and market share are observable and comparative. The gains from a more disciplined strategy may arrive later, while a loss of relative position can be apparent quickly. This difference in timing helps explain why acknowledging a problem may not be enough to change behaviour.

The resulting coordination problem is central to the preprint. Each firm’s decision depends partly on what it expects its rival to do and how others will interpret its own performance. A recommendation to improve margins has to confront that setting rather than assume that recognising an inefficiency removes it.
