RESEARCH DOSSIER · 2026

SKILLFLOW

Dynamic contracting for vocational human capital finance under asymmetric information.

Publication stage & contribution

Working research

Solo author

A loan before the asset exists

Vocational lending asks a lender to assume exposure before the financed human capital has demonstrated its value. Attendance, completion, certification, placement and income emerge sequentially. My paper treats that timing as a contract-design problem.

The framework asks how a financing contract could respond as information becomes available, rather than treating the borrower’s prospects as fully knowable at origination.

The proposed architecture

SKILLFLOW combines conditional sanction, staged disbursement, milestone-linked verification, updating beliefs about repayment capacity, provider accountability and income-aligned repayment activation.

The conceptual method draws on human-capital theory, signalling, asymmetric information, credit rationing, outcome-based finance and vocational-transition research. It compares what conventional skill loans, guarantees and income-share arrangements address—and what their timing can leave unresolved.

Contribution and boundary

The proposed contribution is a framework for sequencing exposure around information revealed during training. The paper distinguishes borrower-level vocational credit from social impact bonds or pay-for-success contracts.

This is conceptual research. It does not claim a completed lending pilot, a measured default reduction or demonstrated employment effects. The next empirical question is how such an architecture would perform under a specific institutional design.

The sequence inside the proposed contract

The framework begins with conditional sanction, then separates that decision from the release of the full amount. Attendance, completion, certification, placement and income provide a sequence of potential signals. Milestone verification would inform further disbursement and updating of the lender’s assessment, while repayment activation would be connected with income.

Provider accountability is part of the architecture because the borrower is not the only actor affecting the outcome. A contract has to consider what a training provider is responsible for and how the relevant signals will be verified. The framework brings those responsibilities into the discussion of credit quality.

Why the alternatives solve different problems

A guarantee can reduce a lender’s loss after a poor outcome, but does not by itself create information at the beginning of training. An income-share arrangement can change repayment timing and the connection with earnings, but raises its own questions about selection and coverage. A grant removes a borrower’s debt obligation, while changing who finances and bears the risk of the training.

SKILLFLOW draws on these distinctions to focus on exposure timing. The proposal is not simply to soften the repayment calendar after disbursing a conventional loan. It is to consider how a contract could respond as information about human-capital formation becomes available.

What remains to be tested

Milestone verification has a cost. Signals can be incomplete, and completion or certification need not guarantee stable earnings. A workable implementation would therefore need to establish which milestones are informative, how verification is carried out, how provider incentives operate and what happens when a learner’s path is interrupted.

Those questions follow from the architecture, but the present paper is conceptual. It does not supply an operating lending portfolio or an estimate of defaults. The value of the framework is the contract-design problem it defines and the sequence of decisions it proposes for empirical investigation.
