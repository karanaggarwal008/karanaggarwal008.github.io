RESEARCH DOSSIER · 2026

Who gets to participate?

Why Does Voluntary Participation Distort Educational Outcomes? An Institutional Analysis of School Based Decision Making Programs in India.

Publication stage & contribution

Peer-reviewed journal article

Solo author

The question before the curriculum

I arrived at this paper through organising school activities. Programmes with strong content could still produce uneven participation. I wanted to examine a prior question: how do the rules of entry affect whose ability becomes visible?

The study compares an eligibility-based mandatory financial-literacy programme with a voluntary skill-based competition in an Indian secondary school between 2023 and 2025.

Method and unit of attention

This is a qualitative institutional comparison drawing on my direct involvement in the two initiatives. It examines participation funnels, competition density and observed patterns of performance, rather than evaluating curriculum content or long-term learning outcomes.

The analysis considers the non-academic costs of entering: public underperformance, social exposure and reputational risk. These can affect who opts in, even when ability is present.

The argument

The paper argues that voluntary participation can select for willingness to be visible as well as for ability. In the mandatory setting, removing the initial opt-in decision allowed some previously less visible students to emerge as strong performers.

That does not establish that compulsory participation is always preferable. The scope is a grounded comparison, limited by qualitative observation and approximate figures. It offers an institutional explanation to examine further.

Publication record

The article is published in the International Journal for Multidisciplinary Research. DOI: 10.36948/ijfmr.2026.v08i01.66041.

The organising experience and the research now form two connected chapters of my work.

The two participation funnels

In the financial-literacy case, the initial pool was approximately 1,000 eligible students. Roughly 100–150 progressed and around 30 reached the final stages. The voluntary cubing competition attracted approximately 100 students across categories, with the four-by-four category closer to ten. These figures describe different activities and are approximate, so their value is in showing the entry structures rather than furnishing a controlled comparison.

The manuscript records capable students practising informally who did not register for the competition. Reasons included feeling insufficiently good for public competition, questioning whether entry was worthwhile and concern about performing poorly before peers. In the mandatory setting, several quiet or initially nervous students became strong performers.

The mechanism comes before the score

My argument starts with a selection filter. Before a voluntary activity evaluates skill, it asks a student to accept the social exposure of entering. If that cost differs across students, entrants need not represent the full distribution of ability. Observed performance then combines skill with the selection process that determined who appeared.

Removing the opt-in decision changes the pool, but does not guarantee effort or motivation. That is why the paper does not conclude that every activity should become compulsory. Its contribution is a mechanism to investigate: participation rules can affect whose performance becomes observable before instruction or competition begins.

What a stronger test would need

The paper is grounded in direct involvement in a limited school setting. The activities differ in subject matter as well as participation rules, and the figures are approximate. I therefore treat the comparison as an institutional analysis rather than a causal estimate of compulsory participation.

A stronger test would need a design that distinguishes entry rules from the other differences between programmes, records who is eligible and who enters, and evaluates outcomes with appropriate consistency. The current paper identifies the question and the mechanism; it does not supply that controlled design.
