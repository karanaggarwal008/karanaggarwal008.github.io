EDUCATION & OPERATIONS · July–September 2025

Financial Literacy Week

A life skill, a school-wide programme, and a question about who gets the chance to participate.

Finance as a life skill

I helped develop Financial Literacy Week for students in Classes 6–12. The aim was to bring finance into the school day through activities students could engage with: quizzes, interviews, group discussions, seminars and competitions.

The programme reached more than 1,000 students. We coordinated 45 volunteers across 49 classrooms. That scale made the practical details important. A question needed to be clear, a volunteer needed to know their role, and the activities needed to work for students of different ages.

The work behind the timetable

I drove much of the planning and execution. I prepared more than 150 quiz questions, developed presentations and social-media material, and helped produce the school reports. Meetings and discussions were only useful if they became materials someone could use in a classroom.

The wider Deepshikha team supported the programme. Tejas shared finance responsibilities, and Sudeeksha helped with coordination. Volunteers did the work of taking the plan into many rooms at once.

What participation made visible

The experience stayed with me after the week ended. I began comparing structured participation with activities that depended entirely on students opting in. Some capable students avoid public competition because entering carries a social cost. Once participation is built into the activity, a different set of students can become visible.

That observation developed into my paper on voluntary participation and school-based decision-making programmes. The paper is a qualitative institutional analysis, not a measurement of long-term learning gains from this week. The connection matters: organising something gave me a question I could not stop thinking about.

From a large first round to a smaller final group

The participation analysis I later wrote describes roughly 1,000 students entering the financial-literacy activities, around 100–150 progressing to later rounds and about 30 reaching the final stages. These are approximate programme figures. The stages included written quizzes and structured group discussions, so a student’s progress was not determined by just one type of public performance.

Some students who advanced had not been the obvious favourites. Quiet students, students with average academic records and visibly nervous participants could perform strongly once the activity began. At the same time, some remained disengaged. Seeing both responses mattered: broad participation made more ability observable, but it did not make every participant equally interested.

What I carried from organising into research

Preparing 150-plus questions and coordinating volunteers across 49 rooms made me think about the design of an activity before its results were visible. Later, comparing this programme with a voluntary cubing competition shifted my attention to an earlier stage: who had entered at all.

I developed the research around that question, rather than claiming that the week had measured permanent changes in financial behaviour. Programme reach, performance during the activities and long-term learning are different outcomes. The organising experience gave me access to the first two and a reason to investigate the rules that shaped them.
