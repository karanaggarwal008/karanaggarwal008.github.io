HPAIR ASIA CONFERENCE · August 2026

Hanoi, policy & independence

The Best Speaker recognition mattered. So did figuring out a new country by myself.

More than a seat in the room

HPAIR Asia Conference brought me to Hanoi for five days of speakers, executive seminars, policy simulations and conversations. It was also my first international trip alone. Navigating a new place, managing expenses and making ordinary decisions without a familiar person nearby became part of the learning.

The programme moved between the Almaz Convention Center, VinUniversity and the Diplomatic Academy of Vietnam. Being present felt different from the virtual conference I had attended earlier in the year.

Representing a position

In the Dynamic Policy Simulation, I worked from China’s position. I supported lead delegate Siya Punyani while considering how proposals could help ASEAN without ignoring China’s interests. I argued for independent bodies in setting rules and for conditions on technology sharing.

I received the Best Speaker award. The result was meaningful because it came from engaging with the policy problem, amendments and competing positions, not simply delivering a prepared speech.

The earlier virtual challenge

At HPAIR VConf in February, I had been one of four finalists in the NFTE AI Impact Challenge. The task concerned a marketing plan to expand NFTE’s educational initiatives. We drew on an outreach approach similar to Eureka Junior.

The Hanoi Impact Challenge was a separate experience. I participated but did not reach its finalist stage. Those two outcomes belong to their respective events.

People I continued talking to

My HPAIR connections included Parth Raut, Christopher Qiu, Vikrant Singh and Mohammad Ateeb. Some conversations continued after the conference. Ayush Anand had also given me advice and confidence beforehand.

The conference brought recognition, but the trip also gave me a practical kind of independence. I had to make decisions in a place I did not know, and gradually became more comfortable doing it.

Questions I took from the sessions

James Z. Li’s discussion of late-mover advantage challenged the assumption that entering first is automatically best. Chandan Singh’s energy-infrastructure discussion raised a related question: who commits resources before the return is obvious? These stayed with me because they connect strategic choice with the timing of risk.

The policy simulation made another distinction practical. I was supporting Siya Punyani from China’s position, so my contribution had to engage with both regional proposals and the interests of the delegation I represented. The work included arguments about independent rule-setting bodies and conditions on technology sharing.
