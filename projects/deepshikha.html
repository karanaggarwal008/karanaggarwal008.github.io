STUDENT ENTERPRISE · 2025–26

Deepshikha

The school company taught me that a good idea still needs procurement, accounts, permissions and people who turn up.

A company with a school timetable

I was Managing Director of Deepshikha at Delhi Public School Chandigarh from April 2025 to March 2026. We were a 50-member student enterprise with shareholders, products, sales events and money to account for. Our team ran five food, accessories and activity stalls during school days and special occasions. The work had to fit around lessons, school permissions and the availability of the students doing it.

My main responsibilities were finance, marketing and team coordination. I also designed position badges and certificates for the core team. Those were different scales of work, but they belonged to the same job: helping a student company operate as an organisation people could understand and take responsibility for.

How the leadership team divided the work

Sudeeksha Handa was CEO, Vrindarak Bajaj was President, Tejas Singh was CFO and I was Managing Director. Vrindarak usually handled procurement. Tejas and I shared finance work, while Sudeeksha and I shared team coordination. The titles established responsibilities without making the work completely separate.

The four of us were generally supportive of one another. Pulkit Malik, the Vice President, and Rabhya Handa, a volunteer, also took meaningful responsibility. I want their contributions attached to the story because the company’s result came from more people than the senior titles suggest.

Commercial activity with a community connection

We sourced handmade products from a disability centre in Sector 43, Chandigarh. The purchasing relationship gave the people making those products an opportunity to earn through the school company. It made procurement more than a question of what would sell: the choice of supplier also mattered.

We also worked with the Sudhir Reema Dewan Foundation around post-treatment expenses for people undergoing organ transplants. My detailed record describes a month of that collaboration. During our year, the company achieved its highest recorded profit performance and received recognition from the School Director. The commercial result and the community relationships were both part of the work.

Permissions were part of running it

Deepshikha was principally student-run, while the principal approved activities. Jatinder Rana helped with urgent administrative permissions, including permission to carry a phone when the work required it. These practical arrangements were part of making a project function within school.

The shareholders, sales stalls and school setting created several responsibilities at once. Finance work had to sit alongside procurement and marketing, while the people taking part also had their ordinary school commitments. My coordination role connected those parts rather than existing only at the moment of a sale.

Financial Literacy Week became a second kind of project

Through Deepshikha, I developed the concept of treating financial literacy as a core life skill for Classes 6–12 and drove much of the programme’s execution. Its activities included mock interviews, quizzes, group discussions and seminars. The initiative reached more than 1,000 students through 45 volunteers across 49 rooms.

I prepared more than 150 quiz questions, presentations, social-media material and school reports. My activity record describes over 100 hours across two weeks of meetings, organising and discussion. This work grew beyond the company’s stalls and eventually prompted a separate research question about who enters educational activities and whose ability becomes visible.

The work after my own year

After graduation, I continued advising the next Deepshikha team and speaking with juniors including Vrindarak and Pulkit about educational and professional decisions. A proposed Diwali Week was discussed but did not become a completed initiative. Continuing to mentor did not mean that every idea turned into an event.

Deepshikha also became a concrete part of how I explained myself outside school. My later YCP interview focused substantially on the company and IDMUN. I could discuss work already in progress—particularly the financial-literacy programme—rather than relying only on titles or future intentions.
